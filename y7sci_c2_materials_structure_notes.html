2 Materials and their structure

2.1 Solids, liquids and gases

Key words: compressed · flow · hypothesis · matter · particle · pour · property · states of matter · theory · vacuum · vibrate · volume

Matter and the three states

  • Everything you can see and feel is called matter.
  • Scientists sort matter into three groups, called the states of matter: solids, liquids and gases.
  • The three states behave in different ways. The ways they behave are called their properties.

The properties of each state

Can it be compressed (squashed)?nonoyes, very easily

You usually cannot see or feel a gas, but you can sometimes smell one, and you can feel air moving on your face.

From hypothesis to theory

Scientists try to explain what they see. For example:

  • you can smell food cooking in another room
  • some substances get bigger when you heat them
  • liquids such as water change to a gas when you heat them
  • substances change from liquid to solid if you cool them.

A suggestion for an explanation is called a hypothesis. A hypothesis can be tested by doing more investigations. When a hypothesis has been tested and is widely accepted as valid by other scientists, it is called a theory.

The best theory we have for explaining how matter behaves is particle theory.

Particle theory

All matter is made up of tiny particles that are far too small to see. What makes the three states different is how those particles are arranged.

SolidLiquidGas
Arrangementa fixed, regular patterntouching each other, but with no patternfar apart, spread out
How close?tightly packed, touchingtouchinga long way apart
Forces between particlesstrongweaknone
Movementvibrate on the spot, stay in the same placemove past one another, but stay in touchmove about freely in all directions

Explaining the properties with particle theory

Two rules explain almost everything in this topic:

  1. Matter can only flow (be poured) if its particles can move past one another.
  2. Matter can only change volume if its particles can spread out or move closer together.
  • Solids cannot flow. Attractive forces hold the particles in a fixed pattern, so they can only vibrate — they cannot move past one another.
  • Solids and liquids cannot be compressed. Their particles are already touching, so there is no space to squeeze them into.
  • Liquids can flow. The forces between the particles are weak enough to let them move past one another, but still strong enough to hold them together.
  • Gases flow and spread out. There are no attractive forces between the particles, so they move quickly in all directions.
  • Gases can be compressed. The particles are a long way apart, so squashing a gas just pushes them closer together and the gas takes up less space.

No particles at all

A space with no particles in it at all is called a vacuum. A vacuum contains nothing.

  2. Which state of matter can be compressed easily? Gas.
  3. Which state of matter cannot be poured? Solid.
  4. List the properties of solids. They keep the same shape; they take up the same amount of space; they keep the same volume; they cannot be compressed or poured.
  5. Name a property of liquids that they do not share with solids. They can be poured, and they take the shape of their container.
  6. Name a property of gases that they share with liquids. They can flow and be poured.

Think like a scientist — is a sponge a problem for particle theory? A sponge can be squashed, but the solid part of the sponge is not being compressed. A sponge is full of gaps filled with air, and it is the air — a gas, whose particles are far apart — that is being compressed.

2.2 Changes of state

Key words: boil · boiling point · change of state · condensation · condense · evaporation · freeze · measuring cylinder · melt · melting point · meniscus · steam · thermometer · water vapour

The changes of state

ChangeFrom → toWhat it means
meltingsolid → liquidice left in a warm place melts and becomes liquid water
freezingliquid → solidwater put in the freezer freezes and becomes ice
evaporationliquid → gaswater on the ground slowly disappears as it changes to water vapour, an invisible gas
boilingliquid → gasat 100 °C all the water rapidly changes to steam
condensationgas → liquidwater vapour or steam touching something cold changes back to liquid water
  • The temperature at which a solid melts is its melting point.
  • The temperature at which a liquid boils is its boiling point. For water this is 100 °C.
  • The warmer the water, the more quickly it evaporates.
  • Evaporation and boiling are not the same thing. Evaporation happens slowly, at any temperature, from the surface only. Boiling happens at one particular temperature, and all through the liquid.

Measuring volume

  • Use a measuring cylinder.
  • The surface of the liquid curves. That curve is called the meniscus.
  • Read the volume from the bottom of the meniscus, with your eye level with it. If your eye is above or below, you will read the wrong number.

Measuring temperature

  • Use a thermometer.
  • The liquid inside expands as it gets hotter, so it rises up the tube. Read the temperature from the scale, with your eye level with the top of the liquid.

Experiment: measuring the temperature as water is heated

You will need — a beaker, a Bunsen burner, a clamp stand, a gauze, a measuring cylinder, a thermometer, a tripod, a heat-proof mat, a stopwatch.

Safety — wear safety spectacles; take care with hot water.

Method

  1. Measure a known volume of water into the beaker.
  2. Clamp the thermometer with its bulb in the water, not touching the bottom of the beaker.
  3. Measure the starting temperature and record it in a table.
  4. Heat the water.
  5. Measure the temperature every minute until the water is boiling vigorously.
  6. Plot a graph with time on the horizontal axis and temperature on the vertical axis.

Why the thermometer must not rest on the bottom. The bottom of the beaker is in much closer contact with the Bunsen flame than the water is, so it is hotter. Resting the bulb there would measure the temperature of the glass, not the temperature of the water.

What the graph shows. The temperature rises, but not by the same amount each minute — it rises quickly at first and then more slowly. Once the water is boiling, the temperature stops rising and stays at 100 °C, even though heating continues. This is because the energy going in is being used to turn liquid water into a gas, not to make the water hotter.

  • Reading the measuring cylinders (A, B, C): 28 cm³, 54 cm³, 82 cm³.

2.3 Explaining changes of state

Key words: attractive force · expand · heat · energy transferred

Heating a solid — why it expands

  • The particles in a solid sit in a fixed pattern and vibrate.
  • When the solid is heated, heat energy is transferred to the particles.
  • The more energy the particles have, the more they vibrate; the more they vibrate, the more space they take up. So the solid expands.
  • The particles are still held in position by the attractive forces between them, so the solid keeps its shape.

Melting — solid to liquid

  • Heat the solid more strongly and even more energy is transferred to the particles.
  • The particles vibrate so much that the attractive forces can no longer hold them in a fixed pattern.
  • The particles can now slide past one another instead of only vibrating — but the forces are still strong enough to keep them touching. The solid has melted.

Evaporating and boiling — liquid to gas

  • In a liquid, the particles touch each other and are held together weakly.
  • Heat energy makes them move more. Some particles gain enough energy to break the weak attractive forces holding them in.
  • Those particles escape as gas particles, moving freely.

Condensing — gas to liquid

  • Gas particles move freely and spread out; no forces hold them.
  • When gas particles reach a cold surface, some of their heat energy is transferred to that surface.
  • With less energy the particles move less and get closer together, and they form a liquid.

Freezing — liquid to solid

  • Heat energy is transferred from the particles to the surroundings, so the liquid cools.
  • The less energy the particles have, the less able they are to slide past one another.
  • Eventually they have so little energy that they can only vibrate, and they settle into a fixed pattern. The liquid has become a solid.
The one idea behind all of it: a change of state is always a change in how much energy the particles have, and therefore in whether the attractive forces between them can still hold them in place.
Questions from the book — 2.3
  1. Why does a solid expand when it is heated? Energy is transferred to the particles, so they vibrate more. Vibrating more takes up more space, so the solid gets bigger.
  2. Why can solids and liquids not be compressed? Their particles are already touching one another with no spaces between them, so they cannot be squeezed into a smaller space.
  3. Why can liquids and gases flow? Their particles are free to move past each other, so the substance can flow when it is poured.
  4. How does a liquid change to a gas? Heat energy is transferred to the particles so they move more. Some gain enough energy to escape the weak forces holding them together and leave as a gas.
  5. How does a liquid change to a solid? Heat energy is transferred from the particles to the surroundings, so they have less energy, can no longer move past one another, and settle into a fixed pattern.
  6. Steam in a bathroom hits a cold mirror. What happens? Some steam particles hit the cold surface and transfer heat energy to it. With less energy they slow down, move closer together and condense into liquid water on the mirror.
  7. Name the processes A–D on the flow chart. A melting, B boiling (evaporating is also accepted), C freezing, D condensing.

2.4 The water cycle

Key words: atmosphere · groundwater · open water · precipitation · surface run-off · transpiration · water cycle · water vapour

Why it is a cycle

  • Water is vital for all living things; our bodies are made up of at least 60% water.
  • Water on Earth is constantly moving between rivers, lakes, oceans, the atmosphere and the land. It is recycled over and over again in a continuous system called the water cycle.
  • The Earth has been recycling the same water for more than four billion years — you are still using the water that the ancient Greeks and Romans used.

The stages

StageWhat happensParticle explanation
Evaporationenergy from the Sun heats the water in rivers, lakes and oceans, and some liquid water becomes water vapourparticles in the liquid gain enough energy to break free of the forces holding them and change to a gas
Transpirationwater also evaporates into the atmosphere from plantssame as evaporation, but the water leaves through the leaves
Condensationwater vapour rises, cools, and turns back into tiny droplets, forming cloudsthe particles lose energy and can no longer move quickly, so they come back together
Precipitationthe droplets grow too heavy for the air to hold, and fall as rain — or as snow, hail or sleet if it is cold enough—
Collectionthe water lands on rivers, lakes, oceans, plants or ground—

Evapotranspiration is a single word for the two ways water goes up together: evaporation from open water plus transpiration from plants.

Where the fallen water goes

  • Some falls straight onto rivers, lakes and oceans, and evaporates again — the cycle restarts.
  • Some falls on plants, and either evaporates from the leaves or trickles down to the ground, where roots may take it up.
  • Some soaks into the soil and rocks as groundwater. Shallow groundwater moves sideways towards streams and rivers; deeper groundwater refills underground stores.
  • In cold climates the precipitation may build up as snow, ice or glaciers, and melts when temperatures rise.
  • Water that flows straight across the surface of the land into rivers is surface run-off. Heavy run-off carries soil off the land into the rivers, which can silt them up and block them.
Questions from the book — 2.4
  1. What are the different types of precipitation? Rain, hail and snow (sleet too).
  2. How does rain form? Water vapour in the clouds cools and condenses into liquid water. The droplets grow too heavy for the air to hold them, so they fall as rain.
  3. Use particle theory to explain how a pool of water on the road disappears. The particles in the pool touch each other but can move past one another. Energy is transferred to them from the surroundings as the Sun comes out or the temperature rises, so they move more. Some gain enough energy to overcome the weak forces holding them and escape into the atmosphere as water vapour — the pool evaporates.
  4. Where does drinking water come from? It depends where you live: a well, a reservoir, a river or a desalination plant.
  5. What have people done to make sure of a water supply? Boreholes and wells, reservoirs, desalination plants, water treatment plants, and moving water from one area to another.
  6. What do we use water for in our bodies? In the blood, in the digestive system, in the respiratory system, and in every cell.

2.5 Atoms, elements and the Periodic Table

Key words: atom · element · group · metals · nanotube · non-metal · period · symbol · The Periodic Table

What atoms are

  • Over 2000 years ago the Greek philosopher Democritus suggested that if you kept cutting a substance into smaller and smaller pieces, you would reach a piece that could not be cut up any more. He called these pieces atoms; atom means "cannot be divided".
  • We now know atoms really do exist. Some of the larger kinds can even be seen using a scanning tunnelling microscope.
  • Scientists have found 94 kinds of atom that occur naturally in the universe. Another 24 kinds have been made in laboratories, which makes 118 in all.

What an element is

  • A substance made of just one kind of atom is an element.
  • Carbon is made only of carbon atoms; gold only of gold atoms; silver only of silver atoms.
  • Because each type of atom has different properties, different elements have different properties.
  • Since there are 94 naturally occurring kinds of atom, there are 94 naturally occurring elements.

How atoms sit together in an element

The Periodic Table

  • The Periodic Table is the way scientists arrange the elements. The full table holds all 118 known elements; here you only need the first 20.
  • The rows are called periods. The columns are called groups.
  • Reading across a period from left to right, the atoms increase in mass. Hydrogen atoms have the smallest mass, then helium, then lithium, and so on.
  • Elements with similar properties are close together. In this diagram the metals are in yellow boxes and the non-metals are in blue boxes.

2.6 Compounds and formulae

Key words: bonding · compound · formula · sodium chloride

Element or compound?

  • An element is made of only one type of atom.
  • If two or more different types of atom are joined tightly together, the substance is a compound.
  • The chemical term for atoms joining tightly together is bonding.

A compound is nothing like the elements it is made from

When two elements bond, they completely lose the properties of the individual elements and the compound has totally new properties.

SodiumChlorineSodium chloride
a soft grey metal, a solida greenish-yellow gasa white solid
you could not eat ityou could not eat itit is common salt — you may have eaten some today

Naming compounds

  • If the compound contains a metal, the metal comes first in the name.
  • The name of the non-metal is usually changed; the ending often becomes -ide. Sodium and chlorine make sodium chloride, not "sodium chlorine".
  • If the compound contains two elements plus oxygen, the name often ends in -ate. A compound of calcium, carbon and oxygen is calcium carbonate.
  • Di- means two and mon-/mono- means one: carbon dioxide has one carbon atom joined to two oxygen atoms, and carbon monoxide has one carbon atom joined to one oxygen atom.

Formulae

Every compound also has a formula, made from the symbols of the elements bonded in it. The small number written below and to the right of a symbol tells you how many atoms of that element are in the particle. No number means one atom.

Chemical nameFormulaWhat it contains
calcium oxideCaOone calcium atom bonded with one oxygen atom
carbon dioxideCO₂one carbon atom bonded with two oxygen atoms
carbon monoxideCOone carbon atom bonded with one oxygen atom
hydrogen sulfideH₂Stwo hydrogen atoms bonded with one sulfur atom
calcium carbonateCaCO₃one calcium, one carbon and three oxygen atoms
sodium hydroxideNaOHone sodium, one oxygen and one hydrogen atom
Read symbols carefully. C is carbon; Ca is calcium. CO is carbon monoxide; Co would be the metal cobalt.

Particle diagrams

A particle diagram shows which atoms of which elements make up a particle. It is an easy way to tell an element from a compound: if there are different kinds of atom bonded together, it is a compound.

  • CO₂ (carbon dioxide), H₂O (water) and CH₄ (methane) are compounds — each particle contains more than one kind of atom.
  • O₂ (oxygen) is an element — both atoms in the particle are oxygen atoms.

2.7 Compounds and mixtures

Key words: composition · evaporating basin · filings · mineral · mixture · natural emissions · pipe-clay triangle · pure

Iron and sulfur: the classic comparison

  • Mix iron filings and sulfur powder and you get a mixture. Both elements keep all their own properties, so a magnet can pull the iron straight back out.
  • Heat that mixture and the atoms bond to form the compound iron sulfide. It has completely new properties — a magnet will not separate the iron out, because there is no longer any iron in the mixture; the iron atoms are bonded inside the compound.

Mixture or compound?

MixtureCompound

Pure means containing only a single substance. Pure water contains only water. A mixture is not pure: it is made of different kinds of particle mixed together, and it may be a mixture of elements, compounds, or both. There are solid, liquid and gas mixtures.

Air is a mixture

GasRoughly how much of the air
nitrogen78%
oxygen21%
carbon dioxide, argon, water vapour and other gases1%
  • The composition of air varies. The amount of water vapour changes all the time, depending on the weather.
  • The amount of carbon dioxide changes too. Natural emissions add it — animals and plants produce carbon dioxide when they respire. Plants also remove it when they make food. Human activity, especially burning fossil fuels, increases it.
  • Over millions of years the composition of air has changed a great deal; at one time there was much less oxygen in the atmosphere.

Carbon dioxide, nitrogen, oxygen and water are all present — so air contains elements (nitrogen, oxygen) and compounds (carbon dioxide, water) mixed together.

Mineral water is a mixture

  • The label on a bottle of mineral water lists many minerals. There is more than just water in the bottle.
  • The minerals are dissolved in the water, so the mineral water is a solution — and a solution is a kind of mixture.
  • On the label shown, the three most abundant minerals are bicarbonate (248 mg/l), calcium (55 mg/l) and chloride (37 mg/l).

Experiment: is drinking water really a mixture?

You will need — safety glasses, an evaporating basin, a tripod, a pipe-clay triangle, a Bunsen burner, tongs, water.

Method

  1. Put some water in the evaporating basin and heat it until it boils.
  2. Turn the heat down and keep heating gently.
  3. Remove it from the heat when some of the water has evaporated, or as soon as the solution starts to spit.
  4. Leave it to cool. The rest of the water may take a day or two to evaporate.

What you see, and why. A solid residue is left in the basin. It was dissolved in the water all along — the water evaporated away and left it behind. That proves the water was not pure: it was a mixture of water and dissolved substances. (Safety glasses matter here because boiling solutions spit.)
