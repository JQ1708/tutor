Section 1 · The basic economic problem
1.1

The basic economic problem

Scarcity → choice
Finite resources+Infinite wants=SCARCITY→CHOICES

Scarcity affects every economic agent: consumers · producers · workers · governments.

Needs
Essential to survive — food, shelter, clothing
Wants
Non-essential desires — a yacht, a bigger house

💰 Economic good

Scarce → has an opportunity cost to produce → has a price.

e.g. oil, cars, trainers

🌤 Free good

Abundant → zero opportunity cost → no price.

e.g. air, sunlight, seawater

How agents face scarcity

ConsumersLimited income → choose what to buy
ProducersScarce resources → higher costs → choose what to make
WorkersChoose between jobs; better conditions cost the firm resources
GovernmentsLimited tax revenue → choose e.g. hospitals or railways

3 basic questions every economy must answer

What to produce?How to produce?For whom?
⚠ TrapA free good can become an economic good — e.g. drinking water, once pollution makes clean water scarce.
1.2

Factors of production

The 4 resources used to make goods & services
🌍 Land
Natural resources — oil, fish, forests, iron ore
Reward: RENT
👷 Labour
Human effort, mental or physical
Reward: WAGES
🏭 Capital
Man-made resources — machines, tools, factories, computers
Reward: INTEREST
💡 Enterprise
Takes risks + organises the other 3 factors
Reward: PROFIT
Memory hookLand · Labour · Capital · Enterprise → Rent · Wages · Interest · Profit

What changes the quantity or quality of factors?

📚 Education & training
↑ quality of labour
✈️ Migration / birth rate
↑ quantity of labour
🤖 New technology
↑ quality & quantity of capital
🏗 Investment
↑ quantity of capital
📜 Gov. regulation
e.g. opening new land for use
🌋 Disasters / war
↓ quantity of land & capital

More / better factors → PPC shifts outward (see 1.4).

⚠ TrapIf it is man-made, it is capital — even fertiliser, a tractor or a fishing boat. Fish in the sea = land; the boat = capital.
1.3

Opportunity cost

Every choice has a cost

Opportunity cost = the next best alternative given up when a choice is made.

Examples

AgentChoiceOpportunity cost
ConsumerGoes to universityWages from working
WorkerTakes higher-paid job with long commuteFree time
FirmGrows wheat on its landRent from a wind farm
GovernmentSpends on educationHealthcare it could have funded
Why it mattersAgents compare what they gain vs what they give up → helps them make better decisions.
⚠ TrapIt is only the ONE next best option — not all the options given up. And it is not just money.
1.4

Production possibility curve (PPC)

Max output of 2 goods using ALL resources
A On the curveAll resources used → efficient
B InsideUnemployed / wasted resources → inefficient
C OutsideUnattainable with current resources
Outward shift ↗
Economic growth — better education, new technology, immigration, investment
Inward shift ↙
Economic decline — natural disaster, war, emigration
⚠ TrapUnemployment does not shift the PPC — it moves the economy to a point inside it.
Section 2 · The allocation of resources
2.5

Price changes

A shift in D or S → new equilibrium

The 4-step chain (use it in every "explain why price changed")

① Cause→② D or S shifts→③ Shortage / surplus at old price→④ Price moves to new equilibrium

Demand shifts when…

  • Income changes
  • Price of a substitute / complement changes
  • Tastes & fashion, advertising
  • Population changes

Supply shifts when…

  • Costs of production change
  • New technology
  • Weather / natural disasters
  • Indirect tax (S ↓) · subsidy (S ↑)

Price vs sales

Price ↑ → quantity demanded falls (contraction). Price ↓ → quantity demanded rises (extension). How much depends on PED (2.6).

⚠ TrapA change in the good's own price = movement along the curve, never a shift. Only the other factors shift the curve.

2.6

Price elasticity of demand (PED)

How much does QD react to a price change?
PED = % change in quantity demanded% change in price
% change = new − oldold × 100

Worked example

Price £10 → £15, sales 100 → 40

%ΔQD = (40 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100 = −60%
%ΔP = (15 − 10) ÷ 10 × 100 = +50%
PED = −60 ÷ 50 = −1.2 → elastic

PED valueMeaning
> 1Elastic — %ΔQD bigger than %ΔP
< 1Inelastic — %ΔQD smaller than %ΔP
= 1Unitary
0Perfectly inelastic (vertical D)
∞Perfectly elastic (horizontal D)

Determinants — remember SPLAT

SubstitutesMany substitutes → elastic
Proportion of incomeSmall share of income (cheap) → inelastic
Luxury or necessityLuxury → elastic · Necessity → inelastic
AddictivenessAddictive → inelastic
TimeShort run → inelastic · Long run → more elastic

PED & total revenue TR = P × Q

Price ↑Price ↓
ElasticTR fallsTR rises ✓
InelasticTR rises ✓TR falls

To raise revenue: raise price if inelastic, cut price if elastic.

Who uses PED?

FirmsSet prices to maximise revenue
GovernmentsTax inelastic goods (petrol, cigarettes) → high tax revenue
ConsumersElastic → easy to switch; inelastic → must pay more
WorkersJobs making inelastic goods are more secure
⚠ TrapsPED has no % sign and no units. It is always negative (you may ignore the minus). Inelastic ≠ QD stays the same — QD still falls, just by a smaller %.
2.7

Price elasticity of supply (PES)

How much does QS react to a price change?
PES = % change in quantity supplied% change in price

Worked example

Avocado price £0.90 → £1.45, supply 110 → 120 units

%ΔQS = 9.1% · %ΔP = 61% → PES = 9.1 ÷ 61 = 0.15 → very inelastic (trees take time to grow)

PES valueMeaning
> 1Elastic — producers respond a lot
< 1Inelastic — producers can't respond much
= 1Unitary — any straight S line through the origin
0Perfectly inelastic (vertical S) — e.g. stadium seats
∞Perfectly elastic (horizontal S)

Determinants → supply is more elastic if…

🏭 Spare capacity
Idle machines & staff can be used
📦 Stock can be stored
Release stock when price rises
🔄 Factors are mobile
Workers/machines switch easily
🪵 Raw materials available
Easy to get more inputs
⏳ Long time period
Time to expand production
Diagram ruleStraight S line cutting the Price axis = elastic · cutting the Quantity axis = inelastic · through the origin = unitary.
Classic questionManufactured goods = more elastic supply than farm goods: factories can store stock & add shifts quickly; crops take months to grow and rot if stored.
⚠ TrapFinal answer is a number — no %. Show your working for the method mark.
