1 Respiration

1.1 The human respiratory system

Key words: aerobic respiration · air sac · bronchiole · bronchus · cartilage · larynx · respiration · respiratory system · trachea · vocal cords · voicebox · windpipe

Why we need oxygen

Respiration is one of the characteristics shared by all living things. It is a series of chemical reactions that happens inside every living cell.

The kind of respiration that usually happens inside our cells is called aerobic respiration. Aerobic respiration uses oxygen. The cells produce carbon dioxide as a waste product.

The air around you contains oxygen. When you breathe, you take air into your lungs. Some of the oxygen from the air goes into your blood. The blood delivers the oxygen to every cell in the body, so that the cells can use it for respiration. The blood collects the waste carbon dioxide from the cells and takes it back to the lungs.

The organs that help you to take oxygen out of the air, and get rid of carbon dioxide, make up the respiratory system.

The structure of the human respiratory system

In the diagram below, the white spaces are the 'tubes' that air moves through as it goes into and out of your lungs.

Air gets into your body through your mouth or nose. Your mouth and nose both connect to your trachea, which is sometimes called the windpipe.

  • The trachea has strong rings of cartilage around it. These rings keep the trachea open and prevent it collapsing, so air can keep moving in and out of your body. If you put your fingers on the front of your neck and move them downwards, you can feel the rings of cartilage.
  • The trachea branches into two bronchi (singular: bronchus). The bronchi also have cartilage to support them. One bronchus goes to each lung, and carries air deep into the lung.
  • Each bronchus divides into several smaller tubes called bronchioles. These branches allow air to reach deeper into the lungs.
  • The bronchioles end by branching into many tiny structures called air sacs. This is where oxygen goes into the blood and carbon dioxide comes out.
Order of structures, breathing in: nose or mouth → trachea → bronchus → bronchiole → air sac. Breathing out, the order is reversed.

The larynx

Your larynx (voicebox) sits at the top of the trachea. It contains your vocal cords — bands of muscle stretched across the larynx, rather like guitar strings. When these cords vibrate, they make a sound. Changing the cords changes the pitch of the sound.

1.2 Gas exchange

Key words: alveoli · analogy · capillaries · diffusion · expired air · gas exchange · haemoglobin · inspired air · limewater

Air sacs

Seen through a powerful microscope, the lungs are mostly holes. These holes are the air sacs. Another name for them is alveoli.

There are also lots of very tiny blood vessels wrapped around the air sacs. These blood vessels are capillaries.

The structure of an air sac

The wall of an air sac is made of one layer of very thin cells. A blood capillary is pressed tightly against the outside of the alveolus, and the wall of the capillary is also a single layer of very thin cells.

Gas exchange in the air sacs

Inside the air sacs, oxygen from the air goes into the blood and carbon dioxide from the blood goes into the air. This is called gas exchange.

  • The blood arriving at the lungs has come from the organs of the body. The cells there have been respiring, so this blood contains only a small amount of oxygen and a lot of carbon dioxide.
  • The air inside the air sac has come from outside the body, so it contains a lot of oxygen and only a small amount of carbon dioxide.
  • Only two very thin cells separate the air from the blood.

The oxygen particles in the air are a gas, so they move freely. They move easily from the air, through the thin walls, and into the blood. This is called diffusion: the oxygen molecules move from where there are a lot of them (in the air) to where there are fewer of them (in the blood).

When the oxygen gets into the blood it dissolves, then goes into the red blood cells where it combines with haemoglobin.

Carbon dioxide does the opposite. There is a lot of it in the blood and only a small quantity in the air inside the air sac, so carbon dioxide diffuses out of the blood into the air sac.

Why the air sacs are small: many small air sacs give a much larger total surface area than a few large ones of the same total volume, so gases diffuse in and out much faster.

Comparing inspired and expired air

The air you breathe in is called inspired air. The air you breathe out is called expired air.

Limewater turns cloudy when carbon dioxide bubbles through it. In the apparatus below, expired air bubbles through the limewater in one test tube and inspired air bubbles through the limewater in the other.

The limewater that expired air bubbles through goes cloudy first. This shows that expired air contains more carbon dioxide than inspired air.

1.3 Breathing

Key words: breathing · contract · relax

Measuring the air you breathe out

You can measure the volume of air you push out in one breath by filling a bottle with water, turning it upside down in a bowl of water, and breathing out through a tube into the bottle. The expired air pushes water out of the bottle, and a scale marked on the bottle gives the volume.

How breathing works

Air is a gas. The pressure of a gas increases when the volume of its container decreases. Breathing works by changing the volume of the chest cavity.

When you breathe in:

  • The intercostal muscles between the ribs contract (get shorter). This pulls the ribs upwards and outwards.
  • The muscles in the diaphragm contract. This pulls the diaphragm downwards.
  • These two movements make more space inside the chest cavity — they increase its volume.
  • When the volume increases, the pressure inside the chest cavity and lungs decreases.
  • Air moves down through the trachea into the lungs to fill the extra space.

When you breathe out:

  • The intercostal muscles between the ribs relax (return to normal size). This allows the ribs to drop down into their natural position.
  • The muscles in the diaphragm relax. This allows the diaphragm to return to its normal, domed shape.
  • These two movements make less space inside the chest cavity — they decrease its volume.
  • When the volume decreases, the pressure increases, so air is squeezed out of the lungs.

A model of breathing

A syringe with the end cut off, a rubber bung and a balloon can be used to model breathing movements. Pulling the plunger out increases the volume inside the syringe, so the pressure drops and the balloon inflates.

In the model, the balloon represents the lungs, the small hole in the bung represents the trachea, the plunger represents the diaphragm and the syringe barrel represents the rib cage. The model is not perfect — for example, nothing in it represents the rib movements caused by the intercostal muscles.

1.4 Respiration

Key words: glucose · mitochondria

Using energy to stay alive

Our bodies need energy for many different reasons — to move around, to send electrical impulses along neurones, and to keep our bodies warm when it is cold.

All of our energy comes from the food that we eat. Carbohydrates are especially good for giving us energy. When we eat food containing carbohydrates, our digestive system breaks the carbohydrates down to a kind of sugar called glucose. The glucose goes into our blood, and the blood delivers glucose to every cell in the body.

Releasing energy from glucose

The energy in glucose is locked up inside it — glucose is an energy store. Before your cells can use that energy, it has to be released from the glucose.

This is done by tiny structures called mitochondria that are found inside cells. Most cells have many mitochondria inside them.

The mitochondria carry out a chemical reaction called aerobic respiration. Aerobic means that it uses oxygen from the air.

Word equation for aerobic respiration: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water

In this reaction, some of the energy inside the glucose is released. This is done in a very controlled way — just a little energy at a time, just enough for the cell's needs.

  • The reactants are glucose and oxygen.
  • The products are carbon dioxide and water.
  • Cells that use a lot of energy, such as neurones, contain more mitochondria than cells that use little energy, such as cheek cells.

Respiration and heat production

Every time energy is transferred or transformed, some of it is changed to heat energy. In respiration, chemical energy stored in glucose is transferred to other substances so that cells can use it, and some of the energy is changed to heat energy. So respiring cells get a little warmer than their surroundings.

Germinating seeds respire especially quickly, because germinating needs a lot of energy. In the experiment below, a flask of live, germinating peas becomes warmer than an identical flask of dead peas.

A thermogram shows how warm different objects are. A living person shows up much warmer than the chair they are sitting on, because their cells are respiring.

Breathing is not respiration. Breathing is the movement of air into and out of the lungs. Respiration is a series of chemical reactions inside every living cell that releases useful energy from glucose.

1.5 Blood

Key words: antibodies · blood plasma · oxyhaemoglobin · pathogens · red blood cells · white blood cells

Delivering the requirements for respiration

Every cell in your body needs a good supply of glucose and oxygen, and the carbon dioxide and water the cell makes must be taken away. The delivery and removal is done by the blood. The blood moves around the body inside blood vessels, and the heart pumps constantly to keep it moving.

What is blood?

Seen through a microscope, blood is not simply a red liquid.

  • The liquid part of blood is called blood plasma. It is not red at all — it is a very pale yellow.
  • Blood looks red because it contains a great many red blood cells floating in the plasma. An adult has at least 20 trillion red blood cells, about five million in every 1 cm³ of blood.
  • There are also white blood cells. There are far fewer of them, and some kinds are quite a lot bigger than red blood cells. They always have nuclei — red blood cells do not.

Plasma

Plasma is mostly water. The red and white blood cells are transported around the body in the plasma, and many substances are dissolved in it. For example:

  • glucose, carried from the digestive system to every cell
  • carbon dioxide, produced by respiration in every cell and carried to the lungs, where it diffuses out and is breathed out in expired air.

Red blood cells

Red blood cells are very unusual cells. They have no nucleus and no mitochondria. They are full of a red pigment called haemoglobin, which is what makes blood look red.

How the structure of a red blood cell fits its function:

  • There is no nucleus, to make more room for haemoglobin.
  • The cytoplasm contains the red pigment haemoglobin, which carries oxygen.
  • There are no mitochondria, so the cell does not use up the oxygen it is carrying.
  • Red blood cells are small, so they can squeeze through very tiny capillaries and get close to the alveoli in the lungs and to the respiring cells in the body.

Haemoglobin transports oxygen like this:

  • As blood flows through the tiny capillaries next to the alveoli, oxygen diffuses into the blood and into the red blood cells.
  • Inside the red blood cell, the oxygen combines with haemoglobin to form a very bright red compound called oxyhaemoglobin.
  • As the blood travels around the body, it passes cells that are respiring. The oxyhaemoglobin lets go of its oxygen and gives it to those cells.
  • The blood, which has given away most of its oxygen, travels back to the lungs to collect more.

White blood cells

White blood cells always have nuclei, and some kinds are larger than red blood cells.

Some bacteria and viruses cause illness when they get into the body. These bacteria and viruses are called pathogens. White blood cells defend us against pathogens in two different ways.

Phagocytosis. Some kinds of white blood cell change their shape and push their cytoplasm out to make 'fingers' that capture a bacterium. The white blood cell then produces chemicals that kill and digest the bacterium.

Antibodies. Other types of white blood cell produce chemicals called antibodies that kill pathogens. A different kind of antibody is needed for each different kind of pathogen. The antibodies stick onto the pathogen. Sometimes they kill it directly; sometimes they glue lots of pathogens together so that they cannot move, which makes it easy for other white blood cells to capture and kill them.
