5 Reactivity

5.1 Reactivity and displacement reactions

In this topic you will: put metals in order of how reactive they are · use that order to work out what a metal will react with · find out what a displacement reaction is
Key words: reactivity series · displacement · displacement reaction

The reactivity series

  • In Stage 8 you tested metals against oxygen, water (or steam) and dilute acid. Put all those results together and the metals fall into a single order.
  • That order is called the reactivity series. It has the most reactive metals at the top and the least reactive at the bottom.

  • Gold and silver are so unreactive that they are found in the ground as the metal itself, not as a compound.

Displacement reactions

  • Put a clean iron nail in a beaker of copper sulfate solution and two things happen:
  • the blue copper sulfate solution goes a slightly paler colour
  • the nail turns copper coloured — it has become coated with copper
  • The equations:

`` copper sulfate + iron -&gt; iron sulfate + copper CuSO4 + Fe -&gt; FeSO4 + Cu ``

  • Why it happens: iron is more reactive than copper, so the iron has 'pushed out' the copper from the copper sulfate and taken its place, forming iron sulfate.
  • That 'pushing out' is called displacement, so this type of reaction is a displacement reaction.
  • The rule: a more reactive metal can replace a less reactive one in a salt.
  • Run it the other way and nothing happens. A copper nail in iron sulfate gives no reaction, because copper is less reactive than iron — it cannot displace it.
How to answer any displacement question: find both metals in the reactivity series. If the metal you are adding is higher than the metal in the salt, there is a reaction. If it is lower, there is none.

5.2 Using the reactivity series and displacement reactions

In this topic you will: use displacement reactions to identify an unknown metal · learn about some useful displacement reactions
Key words: blast furnace · molten · ores · thermite reaction

Identifying a mystery metal

  • If you know the reactivity series you can work backwards from how a metal behaves to which metal it is.
  • The evidence you can collect: its appearance, and its reactions with water, oxygen and dilute acid — plus displacement reactions with different salt solutions.
  • Safety: you do not know what the metal is, so treat it with care and pick it up using forceps.
  • Each test narrows the field. A metal that fizzes in cold water is near the top; a metal that does nothing in acid is near the bottom; a metal that turns a copper sulfate solution paler is above copper.

The thermite reaction

  • Aluminium is more reactive than iron, so aluminium will displace iron from solid iron oxide if the mixture is heated:

`` aluminium + iron oxide -&gt; aluminium oxide + iron ``

  • This reaction releases a lot of energy — it is an exothermic reaction.
  • It gets so hot that the iron produced is molten (liquid). Iron's melting point is 1535 °C, so this tells you how extreme the temperature is.
  • What it is used for: welding railway rails together. The rails often have to be welded in situ — out on the line, not in a workshop — and the thermite reaction can be carried out anywhere. The iron oxide and aluminium powder react in a container placed on the rails, and the molten iron is shaped to join the two rails.
  • Starting it off: the mixture has to be ignited, using another exothermic reaction — this time between magnesium powder and barium nitrate. That reaction supplies the energy that starts the aluminium/iron oxide reaction.

Displacement using carbon

  • Carbon is not a metal, but it can still displace some metals from their compounds.
  • Carbon will displace zinc, iron, tin and lead from their ores. (An ore is a rock that contains a metal compound.)
  • So carbon sits below magnesium and above zinc in the reactivity series — it beats zinc, iron, tin and lead, but not the metals above them.
  • People discovered this about 3500 years ago: iron ore heated with charcoal at very high temperatures produced molten iron.
  • Today the same displacement reaction is done on a huge scale in a blast furnace:
  • Iron ore and coke (a form of carbon) are added at the top.
  • Air is blown into the furnace to burn the coke and create the high temperatures the reaction needs.
  • The iron is displaced from its ore by the carbon.
  • Molten iron collects at the bottom, where it can be run off.
  • The iron ore is mainly iron oxide, and it reacts with the carbon:

`` iron oxide + carbon -&gt; iron + carbon dioxide ``

Practical: extracting a metal using carbon

  • Method: a spatula of copper oxide in a test tube, a spatula of charcoal powder on top — do not mix the powders. Heat strongly in a Bunsen flame for five minutes, cool, then look where the two layers meet.
  • What you see: pink-brown copper at the boundary between the layers, identified by its distinctive colour.

`` copper oxide + carbon -&gt; copper + carbon dioxide ``

  • What it proves: carbon is more reactive than copper.
  • Keeping the powders in separate layers matters: it means any new substance can only have appeared at the interface, so you know it came from the reaction and was not there already.

5.3 Salts

In this topic you will: explain what a salt is · give examples of salts and their uses · prepare a salt by reacting a metal with an acid · prepare a salt by heating a metal oxide with an acid
Key words: carbonate · chloride · citrates · crystallisation · formula (plural: formulae) · nitrate · salt · sulfate

What is a salt?

  • In everyday life "salt" means the stuff on the table — that one is sodium chloride. In chemistry a salt is a much bigger family, and you have already met several: copper sulfate, silver nitrate, calcium carbonate.
  • Salts are made in enormous quantity: the chemical industry makes hundreds of thousands of tonnes of different salts every day.

Route 1 — metal + acid

  • You have reacted metals with dilute acids before; it is often a good way to make a salt.

`` acid + metal -&gt; salt + hydrogen ``

  • The worked example:

`` zinc + hydrochloric acid -&gt; zinc chloride + hydrogen Zn + 2HCl -&gt; ZnCl2 + H2 ``

  • The catch: the metal has to be reactive enough to displace hydrogen from the acid, but not so reactive that the reaction is dangerous. You would never make sodium chloride by putting sodium into hydrochloric acid — sodium is far too reactive and the reaction would be explosive.

Practical: making the salt zinc sulfate

  1. Pour about 50 cm3 of dilute sulfuric acid into a 250 cm3 beaker.
  2. Add 1–5 g of zinc to the acid.
  3. Once the mixture stops fizzing, pour it into an evaporating dish.
  4. Heat very gently until you see crystals forming at the edge of the solution.
  5. Remove from the heat and leave for a few days for crystals to form. This is crystallisation.
  • Safety: the solution may spit and burn you when heated, so heat gently and wear safety glasses. Use tongs to move the evaporating basin onto a heatproof mat.
  • Getting big crystals: leave the solution to evaporate slowly. Boiling it dry gives small crystals; slow evaporation gives large ones.

Route 2 — metal oxide + acid

  • Some metals are too unreactive to displace hydrogen from an acid — silver and copper, for example. Putting copper in sulfuric acid gets you nothing.
  • The way round it is to start from the metal oxide instead:

`` metal oxide + acid -&gt; salt + water ``

  • The worked example:

`` copper oxide + sulfuric acid -&gt; copper sulfate + water CuO + H2SO4 -&gt; CuSO4 + H2O ``

  • Notice what is missing compared with the metal + acid route: no hydrogen gas. The products are just the salt and water.

Practical: making the salt copper sulfate

  1. Pour about 100 cm3 of dilute sulfuric acid into a 250 cm3 beaker and add black copper oxide powder.
  2. Heat very gently, stirring all the time. Do not boil the acid.
  3. When the mixture turns blue, turn off the heat and let it cool.
  4. Filter the mixture. The filtrate is a solution of copper sulfate. Pour it into an evaporating basin.
  5. Heat very gently on a pipe-clay triangle until crystals form at the edge, then leave for a few days to crystallise.
  • Why filter? To remove the unreacted copper oxide powder. You add copper oxide until no more will react, so some black powder is always left over, and it would spoil the crystals.
  • Colour change as a clue: the mixture going blue tells you copper sulfate is being formed.
  • Swap the acid and you swap the salt: use hydrochloric acid instead and the same method makes copper chloride; use nitric acid and it makes copper nitrate.

5.4 Other ways of making salts

In this topic you will: prepare a salt using an acid and a carbonate · prepare a salt using neutralisation · do risk assessments for practical work · use word and symbol equations
Key words: bases · crystallise · erodes · limestone · neutralisation

Route 3 — acid + carbonate

  • Carbonates are themselves salts — calcium carbonate is a salt, formed from a metal and carbonic acid. But you can use a carbonate to make a different salt by reacting it with another acid.

`` acid + carbonate -&gt; salt + water + carbon dioxide ``

  • Three worked examples, all with the same carbonate:

``` sulfuric acid + calcium carbonate -> calcium sulfate + water + carbon dioxide H2SO4 + CaCO3 -> CaSO4 + H2O + CO2

hydrochloric acid + calcium carbonate -> calcium chloride + water + carbon dioxide 2HCl + CaCO3 -> CaCl2 + H2O + CO2

nitric acid + calcium carbonate -> calcium nitrate + water + carbon dioxide 2HNO3 + CaCO3 -> Ca(NO3)2 + H2O + CO2 ```

  • The give-away for this route is the carbon dioxide. If a reaction fizzes and the gas turns limewater cloudy, a carbonate was one of the reactants.
  • Where it happens outside the lab:
  • Limestone is made of calcium carbonate, so it reacts with acid rain and erodes.
  • Coral skeletons are also calcium carbonate. As the oceans take up more carbon dioxide they become slightly more acidic, and the coral reacts with the acid.

Practical: preparing copper chloride from a carbonate

  1. Place 25 cm3 of hydrochloric acid in a small beaker.
  2. Add a spatula of copper carbonate.
  3. Keep adding copper carbonate until it stops reacting, so a small amount is left unreacted. This is called adding excess copper carbonate, and it makes sure all the acid has reacted.
  4. Filter the mixture. The unreacted copper carbonate is trapped in the filter paper.
  5. Pour the filtrate into an evaporating basin and heat gently to drive off the water.
  6. Stop heating when crystals appear around the edge.
  7. Leave for a few days to cool and crystallise slowly.
  • What you observe: bubbling, as carbon dioxide is given off.
  • What the filtering tells you about solubility: the copper carbonate is insoluble — it is left behind in the filter paper. The copper chloride is soluble — it passes through as a solution.
  • Why excess carbonate is deliberate: leftover solid carbonate can be filtered out, but leftover acid cannot. Adding excess is how you guarantee the acid is used up.

Route 4 — acid + alkali (neutralisation)

  • Alkalis react with acids to neutralise them, and a salt is produced. The other product is water.

`` sodium hydroxide + hydrochloric acid -&gt; sodium chloride + water NaOH + HCl -&gt; NaCl + H2O ``

  • The general equation:

`` acid + alkali -&gt; salt + water ``

  • Bases and alkalis — the difference that gets asked every time:
  • When a metal oxide dissolves in water it forms an alkaline solution.
  • Metal oxides are called bases.
  • Soluble bases form alkalis when they dissolve in water. For example: sodium oxide + water -> sodium hydroxide. Sodium oxide is a base; the sodium hydroxide is an alkali.
  • So: every alkali is a base, but only the soluble bases are alkalis. Iron oxide and copper oxide are bases that are not soluble, so they never form alkalis — but they will still react with acids to make salts (that is Route 2).

Practical: preparing a salt by neutralisation

  1. Place hydrochloric acid in a burette.
  2. Measure 20 cm3 of sodium hydroxide into a small flask.
  3. Add a few drops of universal indicator solution. In the alkali it is blue.
  4. Add the acid from the burette slowly, swirling the flask as you go — so it mixes and reacts before more acid is added.
  5. When the indicator turns green you have a neutral solution.
  6. Add a spatula of charcoal powder and stir. The charcoal takes the green colour out of the solution.
  7. Filter the mixture to remove the charcoal.
  8. Put the filtrate into an evaporating basin and heat gently.
  9. Stop heating when crystals appear around the edge.
  10. Leave for a few days; the water evaporates slowly, leaving crystals of the salt — here, sodium chloride, which forms white cubic crystals.
  • Why the burette: it lets you add the acid a drop at a time near the end point, so you can stop exactly at neutral.
  • Why the charcoal: the indicator has to come out or your salt crystals would be stained green.
  • If you overshoot and add too much acid, add a little more alkali until the indicator goes green again.

5.5 Rearranging atoms

In this topic you will: look at how atoms are rearranged in chemical reactions · learn what happens to the mass of reactants and products · learn what happens to the energy in a reaction · carry out practical work safely
Key words: crucible · endothermic reactions · exothermic reaction · the law of conservation of energy · the law of conservation of mass

What a chemical reaction actually does to atoms

  • In a chemical reaction atoms form new combinations. Atoms on their own may join with others; atoms that were bonded may separate and bond to something else.
  • Take iron and sulfur. The iron and sulfur atoms that were there at the start are still there at the end. They have just rearranged.

`` iron + sulfur -&gt; iron sulfide Fe + S -&gt; FeS ``

  • In a chemical reaction no atoms are lost and no new atoms are produced. The atoms are simply rearranged into new combinations.
  • The consequence you can use in any question: every element in the reactants is still there in the products. Nothing disappears; nothing new appears.

`` magnesium + hydrochloric acid -&gt; magnesium chloride + hydrogen Mg + 2HCl -&gt; MgCl2 + H2 ``

  • Follow the elements through: magnesium goes in as the metal and comes out inside the salt. Hydrogen goes in as part of the acid and comes out as hydrogen gas. Chlorine goes in as part of the acid and comes out inside the salt.

Conservation of mass

  • Atoms have mass. If no atoms are gained or lost, then no mass is gained or lost either.
  • The classroom test: put calcium carbonate in a flask, add hydrochloric acid, stopper the flask and stand it on a top pan balance.
  • One prediction was that the mass would decrease, because one product is a gas and gases are light.
  • Another was that it would increase, because there are two reactants and three products.
  • The correct prediction was that it would stay the same, because the stopper means no atoms can enter or leave the flask.
  • The mass does not change. This is the law of conservation of mass: the mass you begin with is the mass you end with.

  • The mass appears to decrease by 43 g. The law has not been broken:

`` calcium carbonate + hydrochloric acid -&gt; calcium chloride + water + carbon dioxide ``

  • The carbon dioxide escapes into the air because the flask is open, so you cannot measure it. All the elements are still present in the products — remember that water is made from hydrogen and oxygen atoms.
  • The fix: put a stopper in the flask so the gas cannot escape, and the balance reading holds steady.

Another surprising result — the mass that seems to rise

  • Some magnesium is put in a crucible and the crucible plus magnesium is weighed. The crucible is heated carefully, and the lid is lifted from time to time to let the air in. Afterwards it is weighed again.
  • The crucible now contains white ashes, and their mass is more than the mass of the magnesium at the start.
  • People expect the opposite, because the ashes look smaller and they see flames escaping, so it seems as if something has been lost.
  • The word equation explains it:

`` magnesium + oxygen -&gt; magnesium oxide ``

  • The mass increases because oxygen from the air has combined with the magnesium. The oxygen was always there — it just was not on the balance to begin with.
  • The history: the French scientist Antoine Lavoisier did this experiment in 1772. He repeated it many times and always got an increase, and eventually concluded that when something burns it combines with a gas from the air. He found the same gas is involved in respiration, and he named it oxygen.
The pattern behind both surprises: mass only seems to change when a gas enters or leaves without being weighed. Close the system and the law holds every time.

Energy and chemical reactions

  • All chemical reactions involve energy. Energy is used to break bonds in the reactants and released when new bonds form in the products.
  • Exothermic reaction — less energy is needed to break the bonds than is released when the new bonds form, so energy is given out. When metals react with water or acids, energy is released as thermal energy, and sometimes as light, sound or kinetic energy.
  • Example: potassium and water. The potassium catches fire, gets hot enough to melt, burns with a pinkish purple flame, hisses or explodes, and moves across the surface of the water.

`` potassium + water -&gt; potassium hydroxide + hydrogen 2K + 2H2O -&gt; 2KOH + H2 ``

  • Endothermic reactions — more energy is needed to break the bonds than is released when the new bonds form, so these reactions take energy in.
  • Example: sherbet sweets. The reaction happens in your mouth and gives a cool refreshing feeling, because it is taking thermal energy from your mouth.

`` sodium hydrogencarbonate + citric acid -&gt; sodium citrate + water + carbon dioxide ``

  • Either way, there is no overall change in the amount of energy during the reaction, because energy cannot be created or destroyed. This is the law of conservation of energy: energy may be transferred from one form to another, but the same amount remains after the reaction as before.
