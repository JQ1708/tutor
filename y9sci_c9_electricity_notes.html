9 Electricity

9.1 Parallel circuits

In this topic you will: find out the difference between series and parallel circuits · find out how current flows in a parallel circuit
Key words: branches · connected in parallel · connected in series · parallel circuit

Series circuits

  • Series means all the components are connected end-to-end, one after the other. We say the components are connected in series.
  • In a series circuit there is only one path for the current to flow. That is why the current is the same all the way around a series circuit.
  • If the switch in a series circuit is opened, every component stops working at the same time, because the current in the whole circuit stops.
  • So if you want to operate a lamp and a buzzer separately from the same cell, a series circuit is no good — you need a parallel circuit.

Parallel circuits

  • In a parallel circuit there is more than one path for the current to flow. The separate paths are called branches.
  • The name parallel comes from the circuit diagram, because the branches are drawn parallel to each other. Components in the branches are said to be connected in parallel.
  • Current from the cell flows to the branch, and at the branch the current is divided. If the two lamps are the same, the current is divided equally between them.
  • Where the branches join again, the current combines (adds together) again.
The rule for parallel circuits. The current through the cell is equal to the total of the current in all the branches.
  • In that circuit the readings on A1 and A4 are equal — both are outside the branches, so both measure the current through the cell.
  • The readings on A2 and A3 add up to the reading on A1 (and A4).
  • The rule still works when the branches carry different currents.
  • Here 1.0 A + 2.0 A + 0.5 A = 3.5 A, which is the current through the cell.

Finding a missing current

If you know the current through the cell but not the current in one branch, you can calculate the missing one.

The total current is 5.5 A, so the branch currents must add up to 5.5 A. Call the missing current X:

X + 3.0 + 1.5 = 5.5
X + 4.5       = 5.5
X = 5.5 - 4.5
X = 1.0

So the missing current through the buzzer is 1.0 A. As with all calculations, do not forget the unit.

Why parallel circuits are useful

In a parallel circuit the current in one branch can keep flowing even if the current stops in the other branches. This means:

  • components in the same circuit can be switched on and off independently
  • if a component in one branch stops working, the other branches are not affected.
  • Closing S1 lights L1. This does not affect L2, because L2 is on a different branch.
  • Closing S2 lights L2. This does not affect L1, for the same reason.
  • If both lamps are on and L1 stops working, L2 is not affected.

Putting a switch between the cell and the branches lets you control everything at once.

  • For any lamp to light, S4 must be closed. S1, S2 and S3 then control each lamp separately.
  • If all the lamps are on, opening S4 turns them all off.
  • If the lamps are off but S1, S2 and S3 are closed, closing S4 lights them all together.

Everyday examples. Car headlamps are connected in parallel so they can be switched on and off together. A string of lamps is connected in parallel so that, if one lamp fails, the others still light.

Questions from the book — 9.1
  1. Which of these describes the current through the cell in a parallel circuit? C — the current through the cell is the total of the current in each branch.
  2. Four identical lamps in parallel, current through the cell 2.0 A → current through each lamp = 2.0 ÷ 4 = 0.5 A.
  3. Two advantages of parallel over series for four lamps: the lamps can be switched on and off independently, and if one lamp fails the others still light.

9.2 Current and voltage in parallel circuits

In this topic you will: compare current and voltage in series and parallel circuits · describe the effects of adding cells and lamps on current and voltage in circuits
Key words: rating · voltage · voltmeter · volts

What is voltage?

  • Voltage is a measure of the electrical energy in a circuit. It is measured in volts, symbol V.
  • Voltage can measure the electrical energy supplied to a circuit by the cell, battery or power supply.
  • Most cells supply 1.5 V. A battery is two or more cells connected in series; batteries commonly supply 6 V, 9 V or 12 V.
  • The sockets on the walls of buildings supply a mains voltage. Mains here means a supply that comes from a power station or generator. In most countries the mains voltage is between 220 and 240 V; in some countries it is 110 V.
  • We often call the source of energy in a circuit the supply — a cell, a battery, a power supply or the mains.
  • Voltage also measures the energy changed by components. A lamp changes electrical energy into light and thermal energy, and that energy can be measured in volts.
  • Most components have a voltage rating. School lamps are often rated at 3 V or 6 V. The rating tells you the maximum voltage that can be used.

Measuring voltage

MeterWhat it measuresHow it is connected
ammeterthe current flowing through a componentin series with the component
voltmeterthe energy difference either side of a componentin parallel with the component
  • A voltmeter is connected differently from an ammeter, and that difference follows from what each one measures.

Voltage in a series circuit

  • Energy is always conserved, so the energy changed by the components must equal the energy supplied by the cell, battery or power supply.
  • Therefore the voltages across each component in a series circuit add up to the voltage of the supply.
  • All three lamps are identical, so they change the same quantity of energy and have the same voltage: 4 V + 4 V + 4 V = 12 V.
  • When the components are not the same — say a lamp and a buzzer on a 9 V battery — the lamp changes more energy, so the voltage across the lamp (6 V) is higher than across the buzzer (3 V). They still add up to 9 V.
  • Current is the same all the way around a series circuit, because there are no branches. Voltage in a series circuit can be different across different components.

Adding more components in a series circuit

  • The supply voltage is shared between the components, so adding components gives each one a smaller share of the voltage.
CircuitSupplyNumber of identical lampsVoltage across each lamp
first12 V26 V
second12 V62 V
  • Adding more components in series also decreases the current, because as components are added it becomes more difficult for the power supply to push the electrons around the circuit.
  • Adding more cells in series increases the supply voltage. One 1.5 V cell gives 1.5 V; two give 2 × 1.5 V = 3 V; a 12 V battery contains eight 1.5 V cells (8 × 1.5 V = 12 V).
  • Increasing the number of cells in a series circuit will increase the current in the circuit and increase the voltage across each component.

Voltage in a parallel circuit

  • Lamp 1 is connected directly across the terminals of the 1.5 V cell, so the voltage across lamp 1 is 1.5 V.
  • Following the wires from lamp 2, you reach the terminals of the cell without going through lamp 1. So lamp 2 is also connected directly across the cell, and the voltage across it is also 1.5 V.
The rule. The voltages across each of the branches of a parallel circuit are equal to the voltage of the supply.
  • This is true whether or not the components are the same. With a 9 V battery, a lamp branch and a buzzer branch both have 9 V across them, even though the components are different.
  • Compare the two rules: current can be different in different branches, because it can flow along different paths; voltage in all branches is the same.

Adding more components in a parallel circuit

  • Adding more branches gives more paths for the current, and the more paths there are the easier it becomes, so the current through the cell increases.
  • Adding more components to one branch decreases the current in that branch. The voltage across the branch stays the same, so extra components just make it harder for current to flow in that branch.
  • Adding cells to a parallel circuit increases the supply voltage, so it also increases the voltage across each branch, increases the current through the cell, and increases the current through each branch.
Questions from the book — 9.2
  1. An ammeter measures the current through a component and is connected in series with it. A voltmeter measures the voltage across a component and is connected in parallel with it.
  2. Six identical lamps in parallel across a 220 V supply: the voltage across one lamp is 220 V. If the total current is 2.4 A, the current through one lamp is 2.4 ÷ 6 = 0.4 A. Adding two more lamps in new branches increases the total current from the supply.
  3. Ten lamps rated 2.0 V with a 12 V battery: they cannot be connected individually in parallel, because each would get the full 12 V, far above its 2.0 V rating. In series, 12 ÷ 2.0 = 6 lamps will operate properly.
  4. A 9 V battery with a lamp (2 V) and a buzzer in series: the buzzer has 9 − 2 = 7 V across it. Adding another lamp in series decreases the voltage across L1 and decreases the current in the circuit.

9.3 Resistance

In this topic you will: discover how resistance affects current in a circuit · calculate resistance from voltage and current
Key words: filament · ohms · Ohm's law · resistor · resistance

What resistance is

  • Resistance is a measure of how easy or difficult it is for electrons to move through a material.
  • Conductors, such as copper, have very low resistance. Insulators, such as most plastics, have very high resistance.
  • Resistance is measured in ohms, symbol Ω, which is the Greek letter omega.
ExampleResistance
100 m of copper wireabout 0.5–1.0 Ω
1 cm of some plasticsover 1 000 000 000 000 Ω
  • Resistance slows the flow of electrons, so it lowers the current. Copper wire is designed to have very low resistance so that the current is affected as little as possible between the components.
  • The plastic around a copper wire has such high resistance that it does not allow current to flow at all under most circumstances.
  • Some conductors are designed to have a high resistance. Inside some lamps there is a small wire called a filament. Current flowing through the high-resistance filament transfers so much thermal energy that the filament glows and emits light.
The rule to learn. The greater the resistance in a circuit, the smaller the current in the circuit.

Ohm's law

  • Georg Simon Ohm was a scientist who studied resistance. He discovered a link between voltage, current and resistance in wires. The link applies to many electrical components, not only to wires.
Ohm's law: voltage = current × resistance
SymbolQuantityUnit
Vvoltagevolts
Icurrentamps
Rresistanceohms
  • The letter I is used for current because in French current was originally called intensité du courant.
  • So Ohm's law is written V = I × R.

Worked example. A current of 2 A flows through a buzzer and the voltage across it is 10 V. Calculate the resistance.

R = V / I
V = 10 and I = 2, so
R = 10 / 2
R = 5 Ω

Resistors

  • A resistor is an electrical component designed to have a known resistance.
  • Many resistors have coloured bands; the colours form a code that shows the resistance value in ohms.
  • The value of the resistor is usually written beside its circuit symbol — for example 150 Ω. When the value does not matter, or you have to calculate it, the letter R is written beside the symbol instead.
Questions from the book — 9.3
  1. Copper wires have a very low resistance. That makes it very easy for current to flow in copper wires. A resistor of 10 000 Ω has a high resistance.
  2. The unit of resistance is the ohm (Ω). Ohm's law is voltage = current × resistance. The letter I represents current.
  3. R1: 6 ÷ 3 = 2 Ω. R2: 9 ÷ 2 = 4.5 Ω. R3: 1.5 ÷ 0.5 = 3 Ω.
  4. (a) V = I × R = 2 × 6 = 12 V. (b) I = V ÷ R = 12 ÷ 100 = 0.12 A.

9.4 Practical circuits

In this topic you will: draw circuit diagrams that include symbols for cells, switches, resistors, variable resistors, ammeters, voltmeters, lamps and buzzers · make circuits that include some or all of these components
Key words: dimmer · fixed resistor · variable resistor · volume

Variable resistors

  • In many circuits it is useful to be able to change the resistance — in a dimmer switch for a lamp, or in the volume control of a music player.
  • The component used to change resistance is a variable resistor.
  • Dimmer switches contain variable resistors that can be turned to control the brightness of lamps in a room.
  • In audio equipment, volume means the loudness from loudspeakers or headphones. The sliding controls on sound recording equipment are variable resistors for adjusting volume.
  • The circuit symbol for a variable resistor is the same as the one for a fixed resistor, but with an arrow through it.
  • The term fixed resistor is used for the ordinary resistors of Topic 9.3, to avoid confusion with variable resistors.

Controlling the brightness of a lamp

Everyday circuits

  • Fridge light. The switch is opened and closed by the movement of the fridge door. When the door is open the switch is closed; when the door is closed the switch is open — which is why the light comes on when you open the door.
  • Adjustable buzzer. A buzzer can warn you that something has been done incorrectly, and a variable resistor lets you adjust the loudness of that sound.
  • Car door buzzer. Some cars buzz when the driver opens the door while the headlamps are on. The headlamps are connected in parallel so they can be switched on and off together, and the buzzer is part of the same branch as the door switch.
  • S1 controls the headlamps; S2 is controlled by the door (door open → S2 closed).
  • Current cannot flow to S2 unless S1 is closed, so the buzzer only sounds when the headlamps are on.
  • Battery voltage indicator. Cars, trucks and some boats use 12 V batteries that need recharging. A voltmeter is connected in parallel across the battery, with a switch in that branch, so the voltage can be displayed when you want it.

Running a 6 V lamp from a 12 V battery

  • Sometimes a lamp with a low voltage rating is needed in a circuit with a battery of higher voltage. Resistors are used to produce different voltages.
  • If two resistors of equal resistance are connected in series with the battery, the battery voltage is shared equally across each resistor — 6 V across each of them from a 12 V battery.
  • A 6 V lamp can then be safely connected in parallel with one of the resistors. The lamp receives a 6 V supply and works properly.
Questions from the book — 9.4
  1. The circuit symbol for a fixed resistor is a plain rectangle; the symbol for a variable resistor is the same rectangle with an arrow through it.
  2. A circuit with a variable resistor in the lamp's own branch can change the brightness of the lamp while keeping the sound from the buzzer constant.
  3. For one switch to control both lamps together, put that switch between the cell and the branches; to control each lamp's brightness separately, put a variable resistor in each branch.
