8 Rates of reaction

8.1 Measuring the rate of reaction

In this topic you will: learn how the rate of reaction can change · measure the rate of reaction · use graphs to discuss and measure the rate of reaction · explain why the rate of reaction changes
Key words: anomalous result · collecting a gas over water · collisions · gradient · rate of reaction

What "rate of reaction" means

  • When magnesium ribbon is added to dilute sulfuric acid, you can tell a reaction is happening because bubbles of hydrogen gas are given off.
  • Watch the bubbles carefully and you see a pattern: a lot of bubbles quickly at the start, fewer as the reaction goes on, and eventually none at all.
  • So the reaction started quickly, slowed down, and then stopped. How quickly a reaction goes is called the rate of reaction.
The definition to learn. The rate of reaction can be measured by working out how much of one of the products has been made in a given time, or how much of a reactant has been used up in a given time.

The equations for this reaction:

word equationmagnesium + sulfuric acid → magnesium sulfate + hydrogen
symbol equationMg + H₂SO₄ → MgSO₄ + H₂

Choosing what to measure

  • In the magnesium experiment it is difficult to measure how quickly the reactants are used up, and difficult to measure how quickly the magnesium sulfate is formed.
  • The easiest thing to measure is how quickly the hydrogen gas is produced — that is, the volume of gas produced in a particular length of time.
  • To catch the gas, attach a syringe to the top of the flask so that no hydrogen can escape, then read the scale on the syringe at different times.

Three methods you can choose between

MethodWhat you actually measureNotes
gas syringevolume of gas, from the scale on the syringethe flask must be sealed so no gas escapes
top pan balancethe falling mass of the flask as gas escapesonly works if the gas is allowed to leave
collecting a gas over watervolume of gas pushed into a measuring cylinder full of wateruses a trough, a bee-hive shelf and a delivery tube
  • The top pan balance method works with the reaction between calcium carbonate and dilute hydrochloric acid. As carbon dioxide is lost from the flask, the mass of the flask decreases — quickly at first, then more and more slowly.

Anomalous results

  • A result that does not fit the pattern made by the others is called an anomalous result.
  • A graph makes an anomalous result easy to spot, because the point sits off the line that the other points make.
  • What to do about one: repeat the experiment. If you keep getting the same odd value, the pattern really does change there; if the repeat fits the line, the original reading was an error.

Reading a rate off a graph

  • The slope, or gradient, of the line tells you how quickly the reaction is taking place. The steeper the slope, the faster the reaction.
  • The line is steepest at the start — that is when the reaction is fastest.
  • As the line becomes less steep, the reaction is slowing down.
  • When the line levels out, no more product is being formed: the reaction has finished.

Working out an average rate between two times. On a graph of volume of carbon dioxide against time:

A   go up from 10 s on the time axis until you meet the curve
B   go up from 30 s on the time axis until you meet the curve
C   the horizontal line from A across to below B  -> the TIME taken = 20 s
D   the vertical line from C up to B              -> the GAS made  = 20 cm3

average rate = 20 cm3 in 20 s = 1 cm3 per second
  • Do the same between 60 s and 80 s on that graph and you get 6 cm³ in 20 s = 0.3 cm³ per second — less than a third of the earlier rate. The reaction has slowed right down.

Why the rate changes

  • For a chemical reaction to happen, the particles of the reactants have to collide with one another with enough energy to react.
  • At the start there are a lot of unreacted particles, so collisions happen frequently and a lot of product is made in the first 30 seconds.
  • As the reaction goes on, the number of unreacted particles gets lower and lower. The chance of two unreacted particles colliding decreases, so less product forms in each later 30-second period — the rate is slower.
  • Eventually all the particles have reacted, there are no more useful collisions, and the reaction has finished.

8.2 Surface area and the rate of reaction

In this topic you will: investigate the rate of reaction when the surface area of one of the reactants is changed · carry out an investigation using calcium carbonate and dilute hydrochloric acid · consider how changing the shape of a material affects its surface area
Key words: surface area

The magnesium clue

  • Put magnesium ribbon in a Bunsen flame and it reacts very quickly, burning with a bright white flame to form magnesium oxide.
  • Put a large piece of magnesium in the same flame and it does not burn.
  • Put magnesium powder in the flame and it burns much faster than the ribbon.

Same metal, same flame, three different rates. The difference is surface area.

  • Only the magnesium atoms on the surface can make contact with the oxygen in the air and react with it.
  • In a block, most of the atoms are inside, away from the oxygen, so they cannot react.
  • In ribbon, more of the atoms are on the surface, so more of them can react. Ribbon has a larger total surface area than a block of the same mass.
  • Powder of the same mass has a larger total surface area still, so it has the most atoms available to react and the reaction is quickest of all.
The rule: small pieces of a solid always react faster than larger pieces of the same mass. Every time you cut a solid into smaller pieces you increase the total surface area — slices of bread have a greater total surface area than the whole loaf.

The cube activity — where the numbers come from

Take 27 identical small cubes. Call the area of one face of one small cube a.

ArrangementTotal surface areaWhy
stacked into one big 3 × 3 × 3 cube54 × aeach face of the big cube is 9 small faces, and there are 6 faces: 6 × 9a
spread into a 3 × 9 slab78 × amore of the small faces are now on the outside
all 27 cubes separated162 × aevery cube shows all 6 of its faces: 27 × 6a
  • The mass has not changed at all — only the shape. Yet the surface area more than triples.
  • If those cubes were zinc reacting with acid, the rate of reaction would increase at each step, because more atoms are in contact with the acid.

Burning iron — the same idea with a different metal

Form of ironWhat you see
iron nailjust glows as it gets hot
iron woolburns, and pieces fly off
iron filingsburn brightly

Increasing the total surface area increases the rate of reaction, because more iron atoms are in contact with the oxygen in the air.

The marble chip investigation

You compare large and small marble chips (calcium carbonate) reacting with hydrochloric acid, using any of the three methods from topic 8.1.

independent variablethe size of the marble chips — and so their total surface area
dependent variablethe time taken for the reaction (or the volume of gas at each time)
kept the samethe mass of calcium carbonate, and the volume, type and concentration of the acid
  • Those control variables matter because each of them would change the rate on its own. The whole point is to let only surface area vary.
  • Result: the smaller chips give the steeper line and react more quickly. As total surface area increases, the rate of reaction increases.
  • Powdered calcium carbonate would be faster still.
Careful — the trap. A faster reaction does not make more product. With the same mass of calcium carbonate and the same acid, both experiments end up producing the same volume of carbon dioxide; the small chips just get there sooner.

8.3 Temperature and the rate of reaction

In this topic you will: investigate the effect of temperature on the rate of reaction · do a trial run and preliminary work and use them to improve the method · select equipment · plot a graph of results

A reaction that is easy to time

  • Mix hydrochloric acid with a solution of sodium thiosulfate (a salt) and the mixture becomes cloudy.
  • The cloudiness is sulfur. Sulfur is insoluble in water, so it forms a precipitate.
  • That precipitate is opaque — so when you can no longer see through the solution, you know the sulfur has formed.
  • This makes the reaction perfect for rate work: you simply time how long it takes for a black cross under the tube to disappear.

Safety. Sulfur dioxide gas is produced, so the room must be well ventilated. Used solutions go into a container of solid sodium hydrogen carbonate, which reacts with the sulfur dioxide, for the teacher to remove later.

Trial run, preliminary work, investigation

StageWhat it is for
trial runa practice experiment to check you can do it safely and effectively, and to find the problems before they cost you data
preliminary workdeciding which temperatures to use — how big an interval (5 °C? 10 °C? 20 °C?), how wide a range, whether to start at room temperature
the investigationthe real thing, planned using what the first two stages taught you
  • To change the temperature, warm the sodium thiosulfate in a water bath before adding the acid, keeping a stopper in the tube while it warms.

What the results look like

  • The 40 °C line is steeper, so that reaction is faster. The steeper line always shows the faster reaction.
  • Both lines level off at the same height: you do not get any more carbon dioxide at the higher temperature. You get the same volume of gas, in less time.
  • At 50 °C the line would be steeper still, levelling off at the same volume.

Explaining it with particles

  • Particles are moving all the time.
  • When the temperature is increased, the particles move faster. So they collide more often, and with more energy.
  • More collisions, and more of them energetic enough to react, means the higher the temperature, the faster the rate of reaction.
Questions from the book — 8.3

1 How can you tell which line shows the faster reaction? The steeper line shows the faster reaction.

2 What would the line look like at 50 °C? Steeper than the 40 °C line — but it would level off at the same volume of gas.

10 How did you make sure the investigation was a fair test? By using the same volume of sodium thiosulfate and the same volume and type of acid every time. (Repeating readings makes results reliable, not fair — do not confuse the two.)

12 Describe the relationship between temperature and rate. The higher the temperature, the greater the rate of reaction.

8.4 Concentration and the rate of reaction

In this topic you will: investigate the effect of concentration on the rate of reaction · plan an investigation · explain the effect using particle theory · learn about another way to speed up a reaction, using a catalyst
Key words: biological catalysts · catalase · catalyst · decompose · enzymes

The investigation

Here the measurement is turned around: instead of reading the volume every 30 seconds, you time how long it takes to collect 25 cm³ of carbon dioxide in the measuring cylinder.

Making up the five concentrations. Dilute the acid you are given, making 50 cm³ each time:

SolutionAcid / cm³Water / cm³
A1040least concentrated
B2030
C3020
D4010
E500most concentrated
  • Fair test: same mass of marble chips, same volume of acid, same temperature each time.
  • Reliable results: repeat the readings. Repeating is about reliability, not fairness.

What the results look like

  • The high concentration line is steeper, so that reaction is faster.
  • Again both lines level off at the same volume: you do not get any more carbon dioxide from the more concentrated acid. You get the same volume of gas, but more quickly.
  • With an even less concentrated acid the line would be less steep, but it would still level off at the same volume.

Explaining it with particles

  • The higher the concentration of hydrochloric acid, the more acid particles there are in a given amount of space.
  • So there will be more frequent collisions between hydrochloric acid particles and calcium carbonate particles — and a faster reaction.

Catalysts

Manufacturers want products made quickly and cheaply. They can raise the temperature, the concentration, or the surface area — but each costs money:

Way to speed it upThe cost
increase the temperatureenergy is expensive
increase the concentrationmore concentrated reactants are expensive
increase the surface areacrushing solids adds to the cost
A catalyst is a substance that speeds up a reaction, is unchanged by the reaction, and can be used over and over again. Catalysts are used in industry because a catalyst is usually much cheaper than heating the reaction.
  • Remember: speeding a reaction up with any of these does not make any more product. It just makes it more quickly.
  • Many cars have catalytic converters in the exhaust. The catalysts inside speed up reactions that get rid of harmful gases produced in the engine.

The hydrogen peroxide demonstration

hydrogen peroxide  ->  water + oxygen
  • Hydrogen peroxide is a colourless liquid that decomposes to water and oxygen. You can tell it is happening because you see bubbles of oxygen.
  • Left on its own, it breaks down slowly.
  • Add manganese oxide powder and the breakdown happens much faster. At the end the manganese oxide is still there, can be recovered (it is insoluble in water), and works again on a fresh tube — which is exactly what makes it a catalyst.
  • A tube of hydrogen peroxide with nothing added is kept as the control, for comparison.

Biological catalysts

  • Reactions inside the cells of living things are sped up by biological catalysts called enzymes.
  • Enzymes speed up most of the reactions in your body — for example the breakdown of food in your digestive system, so that the small molecules can be absorbed into your blood.
  • Hydrogen peroxide is made as a waste product in many reactions in plant and animal cells. It is poisonous: if it is not broken down quickly into harmless water and oxygen, it will kill living cells.
  • So all living cells contain an enzyme called catalase, which speeds up that breakdown. That is why the fruit, vegetables and meat in the experiment made the hydrogen peroxide fizz so quickly.
  • Biological washing powders, liquids and gels contain enzymes that help break down stains such as food and blood.
