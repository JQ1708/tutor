1 Cells

1.1 Plant cells

Key words: cell · cell membrane · cell wall · cellulose · chlorophyll · chloroplast · cytoplasm · magnify · mitochondria · nucleus · sap vacuole

What cells are

  • If you look at part of a plant through a microscope, you see that it is made of a very large number of tiny "boxes". These are called cells.
  • All living organisms are made of cells.
  • Cells are far too small to see with your eyes alone. A microscope magnifies the view, so the cells look much bigger than they really are.

Using a microscope

  • The eyepiece is the lens you look down.
  • The three objective lenses (low-power, medium-power, high-power) sit on a turntable. Always start with the smallest one over the hole in the stage.
  • The stage is the flat platform the slide sits on; the slide goes with the specimen over the hole.
  • The mirror reflects light up through the hole, so light passes through the specimen.
  • The coarse focussing knob moves the lens a long way; the fine focussing knob moves it a very small amount, for the last sharp adjustment.

Experiment: looking at onion cells

You will need — a microscope, a slide, a cover slip, a piece of onion bulb, tweezers, a small sharp knife, a dropper pipette, water.

Method

  1. Cut a piece of onion about 1 cm square.
  2. Put a small drop of water in the middle of a clean slide.
  3. Very carefully peel the thin layer from the inside of the piece of onion.
  4. Push the layer into the drop of water and spread it as flat as you can.
  5. Gently lower a cover slip over it, trying not to trap air bubbles. (Cover slips break very easily.)
  6. Turn the smallest objective lens over the hole in the stage.
  7. Put the slide on the stage with the onion over the hole.
  8. Look down the eyepiece and slowly turn the focussing knob to move the lens away from the slide until the onion comes into focus.

Why it is done this way

  • The layer must be thin so light can pass through it — a thick piece would look black.
  • The water stops the cells drying out and helps the cover slip lie flat.
  • Focussing by moving the lens away from the slide means you cannot crash the lens into the cover slip and break it.

What you see — neat, brick-shaped cells with clear walls. They are not green, because cells inside an onion bulb get no light, so they have no chloroplasts and no chlorophyll.

1.2 Animal cells

Key words: stain

The parts of an animal cell

  • You are an animal, and your body is made of cells. One estimate is that a person has about 100 trillion of them.
  • Animal cells are like plant cells in three ways: they have a cell membrane, cytoplasm and a nucleus. They also contain mitochondria.

How to tell the two apart in a picture: look for a cell wall. A cell wall gives the cell a regular, straight-sided shape. Animal cells have no wall, so their edges look irregular and rounded.

Experiment: looking at cheek cells

You will need — a microscope, a slide, a cover slip, a cotton bud, methylene blue, a dropper pipette.

Method

  1. Gently rub the cotton bud along the inside of your cheek to collect some loose cells.
  2. Rub the cotton bud on a clean slide.
  3. Add a drop of methylene blue. This is a stain — a dye that colours the cells so they are easier to see.
  4. Carefully lower a cover slip over the drop.
  5. Put the smallest objective lens over the stage, then put the slide on the stage.
  6. Watching from the side, turn the focussing knob until the lens is close to the slide.
  7. Now look down the eyepiece and slowly move the lens upwards until the cells come into focus.
  8. Turn to a larger lens for a more magnified view, and draw one or two cells.

Why a stain is used — cheek cells are almost colourless and very thin, so without a stain there is too little contrast to see anything. The dye makes the nucleus stand out most strongly.

Why you watch from the side in step 6 — so that you can see how close the lens is and stop before it touches the slide.

1.3 Specialised cells

Key words: absorb · adapted · axon · capillary · cilia · ciliated cell · dendrite · function · haemoglobin · mucus · neurone · palisade cell · pigment · red blood cell · root hair cell · specialised

Structure and function

  • The function of a cell is the job it does.
  • Not all cells are the same. Each kind of cell is specialised: it has a structure that helps it do its function really well. We say the cell is adapted to carry out its function.
  • When you are asked to explain an adaptation, always join the two halves: this structure → so it can do this job.

Three specialised animal cells

Red blood cell — transports oxygen around the body.

Specialised structureHow it helps
cytoplasm packed with a red pigment called haemoglobinhaemoglobin carries the oxygen
no nucleusleaves more room for haemoglobin, so it carries more oxygen
smaller than most other cellsit can squeeze through the tiny blood vessels called capillaries and reach every part of the body

Neurone — carries electrical signals from one part of the body to another, so that different parts of the body can communicate. For example, a neurone can carry a signal from the brain to a muscle to make it move.

Specialised structureHow it helps
a very long axon — a long strand of cytoplasmelectrical signals travel a long distance very quickly
dendrites — short strands of cytoplasmthey collect electrical signals from other nearby nerve cells

Ciliated cell — stops dust and bacteria getting into the lungs.

  • Cilia are tiny threads, like microscopic hairs, along one edge of the cell. They can move.
  • Ciliated cells line the tubes leading from your mouth down to your lungs. Other cells in that lining make a sticky substance called mucus.
  • When you breathe in, the mucus traps dust and bacteria. The cilia all wave together and sweep the mucus up to the back of your mouth, and you swallow it.

Two specialised plant cells

Root hair cell — absorbs water and mineral ions from the soil. It is found on the outside of a plant root. It has a long, thin extension from one side, which lets water move easily from the soil into the cell. Root hair cells contain no chloroplasts, because roots are underground where there is no light, so they cannot photosynthesise anyway.

Palisade cell — makes food by photosynthesis. It is found in the leaves. It contains a lot of chloroplasts, which hold chlorophyll. The chlorophyll absorbs energy from sunlight, and the plant uses that energy to make food.

1.4 Cells, tissues and organs

Key words: ciliated epithelium · lower epidermis · onion epidermis · organ · organ system · organism · palisade layer · spongy layer · tissue · upper epidermis

From cells to organisms

Living things are called organisms. Their bodies are built up in levels:

cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism

WordMeaningExample
cellthe smallest unit a living thing is made ofa ciliated cell
tissuea group of similar cells that all work together to carry out a particular functionciliated epithelium
organa structure made of several different kinds of tissue, working togetherthe leaf, the brain, the stomach
organ systema set of organs that all work together to carry out the same functionthe digestive system
organisma living thing; it may contain many organ systems, organs and tissuesa human, an onion plant

Two tissues

  • Onion epidermis — a plant tissue that covers the surface of the layers inside an onion.
  • Ciliated epithelium — the animal tissue that lines the tubes leading down to the lungs. All the cilia wave together, like grass in the wind, sweeping mucus (with the dust and bacteria trapped in it) away from the lungs.

A leaf is an organ

A leaf is a plant organ, because it is made of several different tissues:

  • upper epidermis — the covering layer on top
  • palisade layer — tightly packed palisade cells, full of chloroplasts, where most photosynthesis happens
  • spongy layer — loosely packed cells with air spaces between them
  • lower epidermis — the covering layer underneath

Hydra — a two-layer animal

Hydra is a tiny animal that lives in freshwater ponds. It catches even smaller animals with its tentacles and pushes them into its mouth. The mouth opens into a digestive cavity, where digestion happens. Its whole body is only two layers of cells thick: an outer layer of cells and an inner layer of cells.

  • The inner and outer layers are tissues — many similar cells working together.
  • A tentacle is an organ — it is made of more than one kind of tissue working together.
  • Hydra has no digestive system. Digestion happens in a single cavity, not in a set of several organs working together, so there is nothing for a system to be made of.

Hydra has specialised sting cells that help it catch food. Each one contains a tiny coiled thread. When a prey animal touches the trigger, the thread shoots out and wraps around the prey. Some threads carry poisonous chemicals that kill the prey.

  • A sting cell is a single cell, not a tissue or an organ.
  • It has the parts you would expect in most animal cells: a cell membrane, cytoplasm and a nucleus.
  • You can tell it is an animal cell because it has no cell wall, no chloroplasts and no large sap vacuole.
  • It is adapted to its function: the thread is coiled tightly so it can shoot out a long way very quickly, and the trigger on the outside means the cell only fires when prey actually touches it.
